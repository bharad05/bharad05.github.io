About

I am a PhD researcher and Graduate Student Researcher (GSR) in Civil and Environmental Engineering at UC Davis, advised by Prof. Alvar Escriva-Bou. My broader research lies at the intersection of environmental economics, climate finance, and water resources systems engineering. My dissertation focuses on drought risk and explores how economic and financial tools can strengthen the resilience of water systems and support climate adaptation in an increasingly uncertain future.

My research explores how hydrologic uncertainty evolves into institutional, financial, and policy risk in water-scarce regions such as California. I integrate water resources modeling, climate science, environmental economics, and risk analysis to translate complex datasets into actionable insights for drought planning and climate adaptation. More broadly, I am interested in developing decision-support frameworks, financial instruments, and market-based approaches that enable governments, utilities, and communities to manage drought proactively rather than simply respond to its impacts.

Experiences across research, engineering, and international development have shaped my interest in developing practical solutions to water-related risks. I earned my Bachelor's degree in Civil Engineering from Jawaharlal Nehru Technological University Hyderabad (JNTUH) before working with the Morobe Development Foundation on United Nations-supported watershed and environmental management initiatives in Papua New Guinea. I later completed my Master's degree at University College London (UCL), where I continued as a Graduate Student Researcher, developing optimization models for water quality trading while evaluating their economic and policy implications for farming communities in the United Kingdom. During this time, I also contributed to research on sustainable housing and urban development in Greater London. These experiences reinforced my belief that addressing water challenges requires integrating engineering with economics, finance, and policy. My long-term goal is to develop practical, evidence-based solutions that make drought risk more measurable, water systems more resilient, and climate adaptation more effective for the communities and institutions that depend on them.

Water Quality Trading and Market-Based Environmental Policy

Project Overview

Agricultural runoff is one of the largest sources of freshwater pollution worldwide, threatening ecosystems, drinking water supplies, food production, and long-term environmental sustainability. While governments have traditionally relied on regulations to reduce nutrient pollution, these approaches often impose significant costs on farmers and can be difficult to implement effectively at watershed scales.

This research project investigated whether Water Quality Trading (WQT)—a market-based environmental policy—can provide a more effective way of achieving water quality goals while maintaining agricultural productivity and supporting rural economies. Using the River Alde catchment in Suffolk, United Kingdom, we developed optimization-based decision-support models that combine environmental economics, systems engineering, and water policy to evaluate how financial incentives can encourage farmers to collectively reduce nitrogen pollution at the lowest possible economic cost.

Rather than viewing pollution control solely as an engineering problem, this project reframed it as a systems problem requiring the integration of engineering, economics, policy, and environmental science. Across four peer-reviewed publications, the research progressed from developing one of the first optimization-based Water Quality Trading frameworks for an agricultural catchment in the United Kingdom to testing policy scenarios, evaluating economic outcomes, and critically assessing the institutional and regulatory challenges that determine whether environmental markets succeed in practice.

Research Innovations

  • Developed one of the first optimization-based Water Quality Trading frameworks for managing agricultural non-point source pollution within a UK river catchment.
  • Demonstrated how collaborative pollution trading can simultaneously reduce nitrogen pollution, lower compliance costs, and improve overall economic outcomes across an entire watershed.
  • Evaluated multiple regulatory and management scenarios to understand how policy design influences farmer behaviour, environmental performance, and market efficiency.
  • Identified the technical, economic, and institutional barriers limiting large-scale implementation, including uncertainty in pollution monitoring, transaction costs, market participation, and regulatory oversight.
  • Proposed next-generation Water Quality Trading frameworks incorporating AI-assisted environmental monitoring, digital verification systems, and strengthened governance.

Key Findings

Across four studies, the research demonstrates that environmental protection and economic development do not need to be competing objectives. Well-designed market-based policies can reduce agricultural pollution more efficiently than conventional command-and-control regulation while creating incentives for collaboration among farmers and regulators. However, the research also shows that Water Quality Trading is not a universal solution. Long-term success depends on credible monitoring systems, transparent governance, strong regulatory oversight, and equitable participation among stakeholders.

Why It Matters

Water quality is becoming one of the defining environmental challenges of the twenty-first century. As agriculture faces increasing pressure from population growth, climate change, and stricter environmental standards, policymakers require solutions that protect both freshwater ecosystems and food production. This research provides an evidence-based framework for designing market-based environmental policies that support sustainable agriculture, improve water quality, and strengthen long-term water security.

The project contributes to international discussions surrounding the future of environmental markets and demonstrates how engineering, environmental economics, and public policy can be integrated to develop smarter, more adaptive solutions for managing water resources under increasing environmental uncertainty.
